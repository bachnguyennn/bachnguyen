TL;DR

A four-model bake-off for hourly electricity forecasting; gradient-boosted trees on lag features win at 0.81% MAPE — exactly what the data's auto-regressive structure predicted.

# 01 — the question I wanted to answer
In [2]:

Electricity can't be stored cheaply, so grid operators have to predict demand before it happens — a forecast off by a few percent means wasted generation or a shortfall. The practical question: how accurately can I forecast hourly demand a day ahead, and which model wins — a classic statistical model, gradient boosting, or deep learning?

Rather than assume deep learning is best, I put four very different models on the same footing and let validation decide.

# 02 — getting to know the data
In [3]:

I used the PJM hourly consumption dataset. The structure of demand is striking and regular:

  • Strong daily & weekly seasonality — demand rises and falls with the working day and dips on weekends.
  • Intensely auto-regressive — this hour's load looks a lot like last hour's, with clear echoes at 24h and 168h.

That basically wrote the feature engineering: lag features (1h, 24h, 168h), rolling statistics, and cyclical time encodings should carry most of the signal — with holiday flags for the irregular dips.

# 03 — how I thought about it
In [4]:

Two decisions mattered most:

  1. Validation that can't cheat — a random split leaks the future into the past, so I used 3-fold walk-forward validation: train on the past, test on the next 30 days, roll forward.
  2. A fair, diverse bake-off — Prophet (statistical baseline), XGBoost (boosted trees on lag features), LSTM (recurrent net), and a Temporal Fusion Transformer (modern multi-horizon deep model).

XGBoost lands at 0.81% MAPE because lag_1h directly captures the auto-regressive structure. Prophet captures the broad shape but misses the sharp hourly turns; the TFT underfit on a deliberately short training budget — a reminder that a more powerful model isn't automatically a better one. A SHAP check confirmed the short-term lag features dominate.

# 05 — the honest verdict
Out[7]:
verdict: For this problem, boosted trees on well-engineered lag features beat the fancier deep models — and the EDA told me why before I trained anything. The biggest lever wasn't model choice, it was honest validation and features that respect the data's structure. Next step: folding in weather data, the obvious missing driver of demand.
