TL;DR

A calibrated xG + Dixon-Coles betting engine, backtested against the sharpest line in the market — proving with bootstrap significance that its apparent +5.7% edge is really just noise.

# 01 — the question I wanted to answer
In [2]:

Anyone can fit a model to historical matches and print a profit on the sample they trained on. The real test in sports betting is the closing line — the final odds right before kickoff, after all the sharp money has moved them. Beating the close is the bar that separates a genuine edge from luck.

So I set the hardest possible target: build a full xG-driven betting engine and backtest it against Pinnacle's closing odds, the sharpest price in the market — and report the result honestly, even if the answer is "no."

# 02 — getting to know the data
In [3]:

I worked with StatsBomb open shot data (every shot, with location and a freeze-frame of the defenders) for the shot model, and football-data.co.uk results + closing odds for 760 La Liga matches across 2017–19.

Most shots are bad — the xG distribution is heavily skewed toward near-zero chances, with a long thin tail of genuine opportunities. That shape matters: a model that just predicts "low chance" for everything looks accurate but is useless. It told me early that calibration, not raw accuracy, had to be the goal.

# 03 — how I thought about it
In [4]:

I built the system in layers, each answering the previous layer's weakness:

  1. Shot-level xG model — XGBoost on shot geometry + freeze-frame defensive context, run through Platt calibration so outputs are true probabilities (Brier 0.085 vs StatsBomb's 0.079).
  2. Pre-match forecaster — a Dixon-Coles model for each team's attack/defence strength + home advantage, re-fit every matchday on prior games only (no look-ahead).
  3. Match simulator — turns those strengths into the full joint distribution of scorelines.
  4. Betting layer — Expected Value + fractional Kelly staking on the simulated probabilities.
  5. Walk-forward backtest — the part that actually answers the question.
# 04 — what I found
In [5]:

First, the model is well-calibrated — predicted probabilities track reality closely, sitting right alongside the StatsBomb benchmark. The "0.15" really does mean "scores 15% of the time."

# 05 — the honest verdict
In [8]:

That +5.7% yield looks like a win — and here's the discipline that matters: a 10,000-sample bootstrap shows the confidence interval crosses zero. The "profit" is statistically indistinguishable from luck, and the model's match forecast is actually slightly worse than the market's (Brier 0.605 vs 0.584).

Out[8]:
verdict: No demonstrable edge against the close — and that's the correct answer. An efficient market should be hard to beat; proving that rigorously (closing-line benchmark, walk-forward validation, bootstrap significance) is far more valuable than a cherry-picked profit curve. The headline isn't the point estimate — it's whether you can tell signal from variance.
